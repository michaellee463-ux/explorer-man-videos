## Claims (each confirmed by ≥2 independent sources)
1. **You'd stay conscious for less than 15 seconds** — Scientific American ("unconscious in less than 15 seconds"); Aerospaceweb ("conscious for ten to fifteen seconds"; some studies 9–12 s); ESA's Stefaan de Mey via Space.com/Live Science ("10 to 15 seconds"); NASA astronaut Michael Barratt, UH Engines of Our Ingenuity ("about 12 seconds of useful consciousness"). All ≤15 → "less than / under fifteen seconds".
2. **You would not explode; skin is elastic and holds you together, you'd swell** — Smithsonian NASM ("You would not explode... your skin is elastic. You would stretch, and bloat"); Scientific American ("gross swelling... skin would prevent you from actually bursting"); Aerospaceweb ("does not explode"); Wikipedia Armstrong limit ("tissues and the skin are strong enough not to burst").
3. **Above about 19 km (63,000 ft) water boils at body temperature (37 °C)** — Wikipedia Armstrong limit (18–19 km; US Standard Atmosphere 63,000 ft = 19,202 m; 6.26 kPa; vapour pressure of water at 37 °C 6.3 kPa); UBC ATSC 113 ("62,000 ft (≈19 km)... 6.3 kPa... boiling temperature is 37 °C"); Barratt/UH ("above an altitude of about 63,000 ft"). compute.py: Buck equation gives 6.28 kPa at 37 °C.
4. **That's about twice as high as Everest** — Everest 8,849 m (Britannica; 2020 China–Nepal survey); 19,202 / 8,849 = 2.17 (compute.py) → "twice as high" / "2× higher".
5. **The space station flies about 20 times higher** — NASA ("average altitude of approximately 250 miles"); Space.com ("250 miles (402 km)"; "248 miles (400 km)"). 400 / 19.2 = 20.8 (range 370–420 km → 19–22×; compute.py).
6. **The saliva on your tongue would boil** — Smithsonian NASM ("The saliva on your tongue would start to boil"); Wikipedia Armstrong limit (saliva, tears boil away, not vascular blood); UBC; Live Science. Blood boiling claim deliberately NOT used (sources disagree).
7. **Don't hold your breath: the air in your lungs would expand and could tear them** — Scientific American (air in lungs "expands, tearing the tender gas-exchange tissues... especially grave if you are holding your breath"); Aerospaceweb (holding breath causes "lungs to rupture"); Barratt/UH ("don't hold your breath"); Space.com.
8. **You wouldn't freeze solid instantly; heat leaves the body slowly** — Aerospaceweb ("The body will not instantly freeze... cools rather slowly"); Space.com How cold is space ("Heat loss in space happens slowly"; you'd die of decompression "much more rapidly than they freeze"); Live Science ("freezing, though not immediately").
9. **Deep space is about −270 °C** — Britannica CMB (blackbody at 2.735 K); Space.com ("2.7 K (−459 °F / −270 °C)"). 2.725 K = −270.4 °C (compute.py).
10. **1966: a NASA suit tester's air hose came off inside a vacuum chamber; his last memory was the saliva on his tongue bubbling; it took 87 seconds to get the air back; he recovered with no lasting harm** — Guinness World Records (Jim LeBlanc, 14 Dec 1966, NASA Houston chamber, hose disconnected, ~120,000 ft equivalent, "saliva boiling off his tongue just before he passed out", "87 seconds" to repressurise to 14,000 ft equivalent, "no long-term effects"); UH Engines of Our Ingenuity / Barratt (1966, James LeBlanc, saliva boiling off tongue, "another 87 seconds" to repressurise, recovered and "went home for lunch"); Live Science (LeBlanc quote "saliva on my tongue starting to bubble just before I went unconscious"). Narration says "get the air back" (repressurised — to a 14,000 ft equivalent, i.e. breathable), not "full sea-level pressure". Note: some sources describe a "1965" JSC saliva incident with different timings; we only use the 1966/87 s figures that two sources agree on, and do not give his seconds-to-blackout.
11. **Get back to air within about 90 seconds and you could survive** — Aerospaceweb ("will almost always recover if recompression occurs within 90 seconds"); Scientific American (dogs exposed "for up to 90 seconds always survived"); Wikipedia Armstrong limit (recovery if pressure restored "within 60–90 seconds"). Said as "could survive", not "will".
