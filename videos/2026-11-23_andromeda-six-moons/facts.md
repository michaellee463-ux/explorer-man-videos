## Claims (each confirmed by ≥2 independent sources)
1. **Andromeda is about six times wider than the full Moon in our sky** — NASA Hubble Messier 31 ("in angular size it is six times the apparent diameter of the full Moon"); STScI 2025 release (same); freestarcharts.com ("more than 3.0 x 1.0 degrees… over six times that of the full Moon"); Wikibooks Messier Index M31 (190′ × 60′; "more than six times as wide as the full moon when photographed"). compute.py: 180–190′ ÷ 30–31′ = 5.8–6.3 → "six times".
2. **Full Moon ≈ 0.5° (30–31′)** — Las Cumbres Observatory ("about 31 arcmin or 1/2°"); University of Oregon ASTR 122 (30 arcmin).
3. **On-screen scale is drawn to scale**: Moon 150 px, galaxy long axis 900 px (6 ×), short axis ⅓ of that (3.0 × 1.0°).
4. **Closest big neighbour galaxy** — Astronomy.com ("Meet the Milky Way's neighbor"; "just down the street from the Milky Way"); Space.com article title "Andromeda Galaxy: Facts about our closest galactic neighbor". Said "closest BIG neighbor" because small satellite galaxies (e.g. the Magellanic Clouds) are closer; re-checked by the independent checker.
5. **Around a trillion stars** — Astronomy.com ("around a trillion stars"; "roughly one trillion stars"); Hubble M31 page (its mosaic of 200 million stars is "still a fraction of Andromeda's population"). Second independent source re-checked by the independent checker.
6. **About 2.5 million light-years away** — NASA Hubble M31; STScI; Space.com; Astronomy.com.
7. **Its light left about 2.5 million years ago, long before humans like us existed** — follows from 6. Homo sapiens: Smithsonian ("known remains only date back some 300,000 years"); Natural History Museum London ("more than 300,000 years ago"); Live Science ("around 300,000 years ago"). compute.py: 2.5 M ÷ 0.3 M = 8.3×; on-screen timeline puts "humans like us ~300,000 yrs" at 12% of the bar.
8. **Coming toward us at about 250,000 mph; ~70 miles closer every second** — NASA 2012 Hubble release ("moving toward the Milky Way at about 250,000 miles per hour"); UC Santa Cruz 2012 (same); Wikibooks (100–140 km/s = 223,000–313,000 mph); Space.com/Trimble (~100 km/s). compute.py: 250,000 mph = 69.4 mi/s = 112 km/s → "seventy miles closer every second".
9. **For years scientists said it would crash into the Milky Way in a few billion years** — NASA 2012 ("four billion years from now"); UCSC 2012 ("about 4 billion years"); ESA/Hubble heic2508 ("long-held prediction… in about 4.5 billion years"); The Conversation/UWA ("expected in about 5 billion years"). Narration says "a few billion years" to cover 4–5.
10. **2025 study using Hubble and Gaia: about a 50–50 chance in the next 10 billion years** — ESA/Hubble heic2508, 2 June 2025 ("only a 50-50 chance of the two galaxies colliding within the next 10 billion years"; Hubble + Gaia data); NASA 2025 "Apocalypse When?" (Sawala: "only 50% within the next 10 billion years"); AP/PBS 2 June 2025 ("50-50 chance within the next 10 billion years"); The Conversation/UWA ("about a 50% chance… merge in the next 10 billion years").
11. **Even if they merge, stars almost never hit each other** — The Conversation/UWA ("Stars… separated by enormous distances, so direct collisions are rare"); Space.com/Trimble ("any sort of collision is extremely unlikely").
12. **Naked eye: from a dark sky you see only a faint smudge with a bright centre, about as wide as the Moon; most of it is too faint** — STScI ("seen with the naked eye on a very clear autumn night as a faint cigar-shaped object roughly the apparent angular diameter of our Moon"; "spindle-shaped patch of haze with a bright center"); Space.com ("visible to the naked eye but only when viewed in dark skies"); Astronomy.com ("barely make out a long, fuzzy blob"); ESA/Hubble heic1112f ("several times the size of the full Moon (although much fainter)").
13. **A long-exposure photo shows its full ~6-Moon width** — freestarcharts.com (size from "images and long exposure photographs"); Wikibooks ("more than six times as wide as the full moon when photographed").
14. **One of the farthest things you can see with your own eyes** — Astronomy.com ("the most distant object regularly visible with the naked eye"); Space.com ("the most distant object in the sky that you can see with your unaided eye"); STScI ("the farthest object visible… without the aid of a telescope"). Said as "one of the farthest" (Triangulum, M33, is slightly farther and sometimes visible under exceptional skies).
