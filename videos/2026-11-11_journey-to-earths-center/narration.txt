In 1970, scientists started drilling a hole called the Kola Superdeep Borehole.

12 kilometers sounds enormous. But compared to the whole trip, it's almost nothing.

Shrink the Earth into a globe 1 meter wide, and the deepest hole in history would be about 1 millimeter deep.
More than 99.8% of the way down, no drill has ever reached.
Kola never even left the first layer: the crust, Earth's thin outer skin.
Under the oceans, the crust is only about 5 to 6 kilometers thick.
Under the continents, it's about 30 to 40 kilometers.
Kola was drilled into a continent, so it only made it part of the way through.
Below the crust is the mantle: hot rock that goes all the way down to about 2,900 kilometers.
That's almost halfway to the center.
The mantle is the biggest layer by far. More than 80% of Earth's volume.
At about 2,900 kilometers down, the rock ends, and we hit a sea of liquid metal.
The outer core is molten iron and nickel, about 2,200 kilometers thick.
And it's always moving. As that liquid metal swirls, it creates Earth's magnetic field.
About 5,100 kilometers down, we reach the inner core: a ball of metal with a radius of more than 1,200 kilometers.
It's about 5,000 to 6,000 °C. Roughly as hot as the surface of the Sun.
At that temperature, you might expect it to be liquid too.
But the pressure here is more than 3 million times the air pressure at sea level.
That pressure squeezes the inner core into a solid.
